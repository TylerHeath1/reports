Chapter 1 · The fragmentation

Today, a worker's voice lives in one silo.
The data that could amplify it lives in 47 others.

When a paralegal in Faisalabad logs a worker's complaint into MyVox, that's where it stops. The corporate registry that names the parent company. The legislation database that says which laws apply. The brand's own published whistleblower policy. The audit firm that certified the factory last year. The grievance system the brand swears it operates. They all exist. None of them know about the worker, and the worker has no way to reach them.

Chapter 2 · Community power, made tangible

It starts with one worker. It only works when she's not alone.

They need intermediaries they already trust — paralegals from a Legal Aid Centre, women's café organizers, union members, an NGO field worker they've known for years. The community is the data layer's first translator.

Chapter 4 · What becomes possible

The 28-day campaign collapses to three hours.
And the worker is still in the room while it happens.

This is not a brand-led process. This is not a worker filing a grievance into the void. This is a worker rights advocate co-strategising with worker representatives, querying the converged data layer in their own language, and acting before retaliation arrives.

Loose, multilingual input

▌ Anticipated pushback · steelmanned

"Brands don't even know who's sourcing from those factories. Workers face retaliation. Grievance systems already exist."

All true today. Today, brands use sourcing agents (Li & Fung, Pearl River) to obscure their supplier list. Today, workers who speak up get fired. Today, the average factory has a grievance box that filters every complaint through the supervisor the worker is complaining about. This is the world we're going around, not the one we're building inside of.

The bold move is letting workers add what they see — the labels they're literally sewing — and notifying the brand first so they can confirm or rebut. A serious brand will want to know about an unapproved licensee. A brand that doesn't want to know will reveal itself by that choice.

▌ Self-sustaining business model

Brands pay for what they currently extract for free

Today brands take community data — through audits, through worker voice apps they own, through supplier surveys — and pay nothing to the communities producing it. Power to the Floor flips that arrangement. Brands subscribe to see what their supply chain looks like through the community's eyes. That subscription funds the infrastructure that holds them accountable.

For the first time in the history of global supply chains, the people most exposed to harm have the same data as the people most insulated from it.
That's not a dashboard. That's not a grievance system. That's a redistribution of power, made possible by a layer of data infrastructure that's never existed before.
